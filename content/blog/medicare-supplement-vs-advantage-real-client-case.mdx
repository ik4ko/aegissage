  - question: "If I leave my supplement for Medicare Advantage, can I get it back?"
    answer: "If you dropped a Medigap policy to join a Medicare Advantage plan for the first time, Medicare gives you a trial right: if you leave the Advantage plan within the first year, you can buy the Medigap policy you had before if the same company still sells it, or otherwise one of Plans A, B, C, D, F, G, K or L sold in your state, with no health questions. You apply up to 60 days before and no more than 63 days after your Advantage coverage ends. After that first year, in New Jersey a supplement company can generally ask health questions and turn you down unless you have another guaranteed issue right."
  - question: "What if my Medicare Advantage plan ends or leaves my area?"
    answer: "If your Advantage plan leaves Medicare or stops covering your area and you switch to Original Medicare, you have a guaranteed right to buy a Medigap Plan A, B, C, D, F, G, K or L sold in your state. You only have this right if you go back to Original Medicare rather than joining another Advantage plan."
  - question: "Is it different in New York?"
    answer: "Yes. New York State law requires insurers that sell Medigap to accept a Medicare enrollee's application at any time of year, without denying the policy or charging more because of health. That makes trying a Medicare Advantage plan much less of a one-way door for New York residents."
  - question: "Can doctors leave a Medicare Advantage network?"
    answer: "Yes. Networks and plan benefits can change every year. Your plan has to mail you an Annual Notice of Change each fall, and it is worth checking your doctors and drugs against it every year during open enrollment, October 15 to December 7."
  - question: "Can I see out-of-network doctors on a Medicare Advantage PPO?"
    answer: "Generally yes, for covered services, but you usually pay more. Medicare says the provider must participate in Medicare or accept assignment, and it suggests contacting your plan before an out-of-network visit to make sure the service is covered. In practice, call the doctor's billing office first, because some offices do not want to bill a plan they have no contract with."
  - question: "What is the difference between the in-network and the combined out-of-pocket maximum on a PPO?"
    answer: "The in-network maximum caps what you pay in a year for in-network care. The combined maximum is a higher cap that counts both in-network and out-of-network costs. Seeing an out-of-network doctor once does not switch you to the higher limit: in-network costs count toward both caps, and out-of-network costs count only toward the combined one. For your worst case, look at the combined number."
  - question: "Do Medicare Advantage plans require prior authorization?"
    answer: "Many do, for some services such as certain scans, procedures and therapy. Original Medicare with a supplement generally does not. It is a real tradeoff and belongs in the decision alongside the cost."

Sometimes a Medicare Advantage plan really is the better fit, even for someone who sees a lot of doctors. It depends on that person's doctors, prescriptions, how often they use care, and what they can afford, and the only way to know is to price it out. Here is a real case from this fall, showing what we checked, what she saves, and what she gave up.

*To protect my client's privacy, some details are changed and figures are rounded.*

## The situation

- She was paying **roughly $5,000 to $6,000 a year** for a Medicare supplement plus a Part D drug plan, before she saw a single doctor. And that cost kept going up.
- She sees **a long list of doctors**, many of them specialists.
- She has **physical therapy several times a month**, all year, which comes to roughly 40 visits a year.

Most people would look at that and say "don't touch anything." I understand why. With that much care, a supplement is usually the safe choice. But "safe" stops meaning much when the premium is outgrowing your income.

## What we checked before changing anything

Nothing changed until all of this was done:

1. **Every doctor, one by one,** against the new plan's network.
2. **Every prescription,** against the plan's drug list, including the tier and the pharmacy.
3. **Physical therapy, priced for a full year.** This is the step most people skip. Forty-odd visits times a copay adds up fast. If that number had come out badly, the switch would have been off no matter how low the premium was.
4. **The worst case:** the plan's yearly out-of-pocket maximum. If she had a bad year, what is the most she could pay?
5. **The way back:** what happens if this does not work out. That is covered in the questions at the end.

## The result

| | Before: supplement + Part D | After: Advantage PPO |
|---|---|---|
| A typical year for her | About $5,000 to $6,000, mostly premiums | **Roughly 50 to 70% less**, counting the new premium plus her expected copays for doctors, therapy and drugs |
| A bad year | Very little beyond the premiums | Higher. If she reached the plan's out-of-pocket maximum, her costs could come close to, or go above, what she used to pay |

That bottom row is the honest part. In a normal year for her, she comes out well ahead. In a bad year she carries more risk, and she made that trade knowing it.

Most of her doctors were in the network. The few who were not, she can still see at a higher cost, because it is a PPO. She also found an in-network chiropractor so she would not pay out-of-network prices for the visits she makes most often.

<Callout type="watch" title="Your numbers will be different">
Premiums, copays, networks and drug coverage depend on the plan, where you live and the year. One person's result says nothing about what you would pay. The point of this story is the method, not the number.
</Callout>

## When I tell people to keep their supplement

The same week, someone asked me about their own situation: several specialists, plus infusions costing thousands of dollars each, several times a month. I told them I would usually land on the **supplement side.**

Infusions given at an infusion center or a doctor's office are usually covered under **Part B**, not Part D. With Original Medicare plus a supplement, Part B pays 80% and the supplement covers most or all of the rest. On an Advantage plan, that share is yours until you reach the plan's out-of-pocket maximum, and you also deal with prior authorization and which infusion centers are in network. At that level of care, the supplement premium is worth paying.

<KeyPoints>

- **A supplement often fits better if you:** have very high, ongoing Part B costs such as infusions, chemotherapy or dialysis; need specific doctors or hospital systems that do not take Advantage plans; split the year between states; or can comfortably afford the premium as it rises.
- **Medicare Advantage can fit better if you:** have a supplement premium that is outgrowing your income; have doctors who are in a plan's network; can live with your total yearly cost including the out-of-pocket maximum; and are comfortable with prior authorization.

</KeyPoints>

Neither one is the "smart" choice or the "safe" choice for everyone. More than half of people with Medicare Parts A and B are in Medicare Advantage in 2026, about 35 million people according to KFF, and around 13.8 million people had a Medicare supplement at the end of 2025. Plenty of people are well served by each.

## How to do this math for yourself

1. List every doctor, every prescription, and how often you actually use care, including therapy and regular procedures.
2. Price **a normal year** and **a bad year** under each option.
3. Find out whether you could get back to a supplement if you needed to, and when.
4. Get it in writing. Someone helping you should show you the numbers for both options before recommending either one. If they only sell one kind of plan, or will not show you the math, keep looking.
